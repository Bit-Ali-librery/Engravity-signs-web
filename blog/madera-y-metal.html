When wood meets metal: 5 signs we learned to make

Combining wood and metal in the same sign is one of those things that look gorgeous in the render and turn complicated in production. Wood breathes, metal doesn't. Wood expands with humidity, metal holds its size. And both materials require different tools, speeds, and finishes.

These are five real shop projects where we combined the two materials. Each one with a problem that didn't show up on the drawing — and that we learned to anticipate.

Case 1 — Corporate sign with oak base and aluminum letters

RECEPTION · CORPORATE OFFICE

The problem: uneven thermal expansion

Brushed aluminum letters glued with epoxy onto a solid oak panel. Worked perfectly the first month. After the first season change, several letters started peeling off at the corners.

The wood contracted with the dry winter air. The aluminum didn't. The epoxy couldn't take the difference.

What we learned: When you combine solid wood with metal, avoid rigid adhesives. Use mechanical standoffs or flexible adhesives like VHB that absorb the expansion difference. Or replace solid wood with plywood, which moves less.

Case 2 — ADA restroom with wood backer and bronze plate

ADA SIGN · SHOPPING CENTER

The problem: real contrast

The client wanted an organic look: walnut backer with antiqued bronze front plate and raised text. Visually beautiful. Failed the ADA inspection.

The contrast between the antiqued bronze and the raised text didn't reach the 70% required by ADA 703. What looked elegant in the render failed readability for low-vision users.

What we learned: Validate contrast against ADA standards before approving the design with the client. Dark finishes like antiqued bronze or patinated brass almost always fail contrast if the text is also dark. Engravity's validator warns you before sending to production.

Case 3 — Exterior sign with steel frame and cedar panel

EXTERIOR SIGN · RESTAURANT

The problem: galvanic corrosion

Welded carbon steel frame surrounding a treated cedar panel. After three months of rain, the cedar started to stain black around the screws.

Carbon steel in contact with moisture releases tannins into the wood. Cedar is particularly sensitive to it. Stainless steel screws would have prevented this completely.

What we learned: For exteriors, any hardware touching wood must be stainless or hot-dip galvanized. The cost difference is marginal compared to having to disassemble the sign a year later.

Case 4 — MDF logo with aluminum inlay

INTERIOR SIGNAGE · CLINIC

The problem: different bit tolerances

Pocket cut in MDF to inlay custom-cut aluminum pieces. The bit cut the pocket with "for-wood" tolerance and the aluminum pieces cut with "for-metal" tolerance didn't fit. There was a millimeter of gap on each side.

Bit speeds and offsets are different for each material. When you go from MDF to aluminum, the offset has to be recalculated.

What we learned: When two materials have to interlock, simulate both cuts in the same file and verify that the final dimensions match. Engravity now shows warnings when bit offsets differ across operations within the same project.

Case 5 — Monument sign with steel structure and treated pine panels

MONUMENT SIGN · INDUSTRIAL PARK

The problem: real weight vs. render weight

Designed in pretty 3D software. Sent to fabrication. We realized too late that pressure-treated pine panels (with anti-mold chemicals) weigh almost 35% more than regular dry pine. The steel structure was calculated for dry weight. We had to reinforce it on site.

Design software vendors don't include real densities for treated materials. The render looks the same, but reality weighs more.

What we learned: Before approving the structure, calculate weight using treated-material densities, not dry-material ones. For pressure-treated pine use 35–40% more than dry pine. For thermally treated oak, subtract 15–20%.

The pattern behind the 5 cases

All these problems share something: they show up when two materials with different behaviors meet. The aesthetic of the wood-metal contrast is exactly what makes them tough to produce.

The four rules we took away are:

  1. Wood moves. Design with that in mind, not against it.
  2. Visual contrast is not ADA contrast. Verify with numbers, not with your eye.
  3. Outdoor hardware is stainless or nothing. No negotiation.
  4. Tolerances are per material, not per file. Recalculate when you switch materials, even within the same project.

If you combine these two materials often, write to us. We're collecting cases for a more extensive technical guide.
